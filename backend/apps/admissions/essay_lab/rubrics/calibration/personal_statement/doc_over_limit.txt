Every Saturday from the age of twelve I sold mobile phone cases at my uncle's stall in the Abu Saxiy market in Tashkent. The stall was two metres wide and had a metal shutter that screamed when you opened it. My uncle said the shutter was our alarm clock, because nobody on the row could sleep through it.

At first my only job was to keep the cases in order by phone model. There were more than two hundred models, and customers never knew which one they had. I learned to recognise a Redmi Note 8 from a Redmi Note 9 by the shape of the camera bump, and I was proud of it in a way I could not explain to my friends.

When I was fourteen my uncle went to Moscow for work for six months and left the stall to my cousin Jasur and me. Jasur was nineteen and more interested in his phone than in selling phones. In the first month we lost money, because we ordered cases for models nobody in our part of the market used. I remember one box of forty cases for a phone I had never seen a single customer hold.

I started a notebook. Every time a customer asked for a case we did not have, I wrote down the model. After four weeks I had a list of eleven models we had never stocked, and three of them appeared more than twenty times. I showed the list to Jasur, and he said the supplier would not take back the old boxes anyway, so what was the point.

The point, I decided, was the next order. I called the supplier myself, which was the first business call I had ever made, and my voice cracked on the word 'wholesale'. He laughed, but he agreed to sell us smaller batches of the models on my list. By the third month we were making a small profit, and Jasur started to ask me what to order.

I also learned things that were not in the notebook. Old women often brought their grandchildren's phones and wanted the cheapest case, but they always asked which one was strongest. I started to tell them the truth, that the cheapest one cracked at the corners, and most of them paid the extra five thousand som. A man from the next stall told me I was ruining prices for everyone by being honest, and I still do not know if he was joking.

When my uncle came back, he looked at the notebook for a long time. Then he took the forty unsold cases from under the counter and put them on top, at half price, with a sign that said 'rare model'. They were gone in three Saturdays. I had been ashamed of that box for months, and he turned it into a joke and a profit in an afternoon.

That moment changed how I think about mistakes. I had treated the box as proof that we had failed, something to hide under the counter. My uncle treated it as stock that had not found its customer yet. I am not sure his way is always right, because some mistakes really are just losses, but I no longer hide them before I have looked at them twice.

Now I keep a notebook for almost everything: my chemistry practice, the times my younger sister and I argue about the computer, the prices of bread at three different shops near our house. Most of these notebooks are useless. A few of them have shown me a pattern I would not have noticed otherwise, like the fact that I make the same sign mistake in every third chemistry problem when I am tired.

I want to study economics because the market taught me that people's choices are messier and more interesting than the graphs in my textbook. The old women who paid more for a stronger case were not following the cheapest price, and the boys who resold our cases understood arbitrage before I knew the word. I want to learn the tools to understand those choices properly, and I want to keep a notebook while I do it.

The shutter still screams every Saturday. I still open it.
