My grandfather fixed clocks in a room behind our kitchen in Namangan. The room smelled of machine oil and dried apricots, because he kept both on the same shelf. When I was eleven he let me hold the tweezers while he lifted a balance wheel out of a Soviet Pobeda watch, and he told me not to breathe on it. I held my breath so long that I saw spots.

He died in February of my ninth-grade year. For three weeks I did not open the door to his room. My mother asked me twice to sort his tools, and twice I said I had homework, which was partly true.

When I finally went in, the Pobeda was still on the bench, face down, with its back open. He had not finished it. I did not know whose watch it was. There was no name in his notebook, only a date and the word "slow."

I decided to finish it. I watched repair videos at night with the sound off so that nobody would ask what I was doing. I bent a hairspring on my first try and had to order another one from a seller in Tashkent, which took nineteen days. The second time, I wrote down every step in his notebook, under his last line, in pencil, because pen felt like too much.

It took five weeks. When the watch ran, it lost four minutes a day. I adjusted the regulator a hair at a time until it lost thirty seconds. I could not get it closer, and I still do not know if that was my hands or the watch.

In April a neighbour, Rustam aka, knocked on our door and asked about a watch he had left with my grandfather. I gave it to him and told him it was thirty seconds slow. He wound it, held it to his ear, and said that was faster than it had ever been.

I used to think finishing something for someone meant doing it the way they would have. Now I think I finished it my way, slowly, in pencil, and he would have argued with half of my choices.
