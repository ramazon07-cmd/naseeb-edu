Every Friday after the mosque, three or four neighbours knock at our gate with a phone and the same sentence: Rustam, nima bo'ldi buning? What happened to this one? I started repairing phones in the mahalla at fourteen, with a screwdriver set from Chorsu that cost me forty thousand som, and I keep a green school notebook where I write each phone, what I thought was wrong, and what was actually wrong.

The notebook is mostly a list of my mistakes. Page 6: Mrs. Gulbahor's Samsung, "dead battery", actually a dirty charging port, and I had already sold her a battery she did not need. I went back to her house and returned the money, and she gave me a bag of walnuts that I did not want to take. Page 11: the baker's phone that would not turn on. I opened it, swore, and found that his daughter had put a sticker over the speaker. Seven phones in a row I blamed the screen when the fault was the cable. I now check the cable first, which I would never have learned if the first six guesses had been right.

I used to think a good repairer is someone who is right. Now I think it is someone who knows how often he is wrong, and writes it down.

The phone I cannot stop thinking about arrived in March. A man from the next street brought his late wife's phone. It would not charge, and there were four thousand photos on it that he had never copied. I replaced the port and it started. Then he asked me not to open the gallery, and I nodded, and I do not know what I would have done if he had not asked. I had the phone in my hands for two hours and the photos were one tap away. I did not look. I still do not know whether that was respect, or only fear of what I might see.

In my notebook that page has the date, the model, the word "port", and nothing else.
