At eleven the night before the regional robotics event, the left drive motor of our robot gave off a thin line of smoke. I was alone in the school workshop with the key Mr. Karimov had lent me. Our team of four had gone home at nine because I told them I could finish the wiring alone.

I did not call anyone. For two years I had believed that a captain who asks for help has not prepared. I took the motor apart, found two brush wires touching, and spent an hour rebuilding it with a wire from a phone charger. At one in the morning it ran for nine seconds and stopped. I sat on the floor and counted what I had: no spare motor, a bus at seven, and three friends whose phones I had never dared to wake.

At 1:20 I wrote to Dilnoza, who had joined in September and whom I had mostly given the boring tasks. Ten minutes later she answered with a photo of a motor on her desk, the one from her brother's drone. She cycled to school at half past six with it in her backpack, and Jasur, whom I had thought could only solder, fitted it on the bus.

We came ninth out of twenty-two. It was not a win, and the new motor had a different gearing, so the robot pulled left all day. Nobody blamed me for it.

I still catch myself doing things alone. But when I plan a task now, I write the names of two people next to it before I write my own. I am not sure yet whether this makes me a better captain, or only a less tired one.
