To the Admissions Committee,

I have taught Malika Karimova physics for three years, from grade 9 to grade 11, in classes of about thirty students. Over that time I have taught roughly 120 students in her year, and I would place her among the strongest of them in experimental work, though not the fastest at written tests.

One afternoon in October of grade 10 stays with me. We were measuring the acceleration of a cart on an inclined track, and every group's results came out about fifteen percent too low. Most students wrote "friction" in their conclusions and moved on. Malika did not. She asked if she could stay after the bell, and for forty minutes she timed the cart with three different stopwatches, then with her phone's slow-motion camera. She came to my desk with a page of numbers and said, "I think the problem is us, not the track. We press the stopwatch late." She was right: our reaction time was adding almost a tenth of a second to every run. The next week she showed the class how to film the cart and count frames, and the whole class's results moved to within three percent of the expected value.

What I noticed was not only that she found the error. It was that she was more interested in why the measurement was wrong than in getting a good mark for the lab. She treats a bad result as information.

She is not a loud student. In group work she usually lets others speak first, and in grade 9 I sometimes worried that her ideas went unheard. By grade 11 she had found a way to make her point with a sketch or a number rather than with volume, and other students started to wait for her sketch.

Her written test results are good but uneven; she sometimes loses marks for rushing the last question. I mention this because it is the one area where I have seen her improve slowly, and because I think a reader should know that her strength is in the laboratory, where she has time to look twice.

Malika wants to study physics or engineering. Based on three years of watching her work, I believe she will be the student in a lab who notices that the method, and not the world, is wrong. I recommend her with confidence.

Sincerely,
Physics teacher
