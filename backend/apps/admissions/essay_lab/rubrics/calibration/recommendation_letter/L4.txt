To the Admissions Committee,

I have been Malika Karimova's school counselor for three years, since she entered grade 9. Our school is a state general-education school with about 900 students. A few facts may help a reader abroad. Uzbek schools grade on a five-point scale, where 5 is excellent and 3 is a pass; a 5 in physics or mathematics is given to perhaps one or two students in a class of thirty. Students study fourteen or more subjects each year, all compulsory, so there are no advanced or honours courses to choose. Malika has earned 5 in mathematics and physics every term since grade 9 and 4 in Russian literature.

Within that context, I would describe her as one of the three or four students in her year of about 110 whom teachers send to me when a younger student needs help with mathematics. I know her mostly through that tutoring, through our weekly planning meetings in grade 11, and through two years of the school's science fair.

The first moment I want to describe I saw myself. In March of grade 10, a grade 7 boy came to her for help with fractions after failing two tests. I was in the room. Instead of explaining, she asked him to cut an apple into pieces with a plastic knife and to tell her which piece was bigger, a third or a quarter. He said a quarter, because four is bigger than three. She did not correct him. She handed him the two pieces and waited. He looked at them for a long time and laughed. Over the next six weeks she met him every Tuesday, and he passed his next test with a 4. When I asked her afterwards why she had not simply told him the answer, she said that he would not have believed her, only the apple.

The second moment comes from her chemistry teacher, Ms Nodira Saidova, who told me about it the same week it happened. During a practical in grade 11, Malika's group was told to stop because time was up, and their titration result was clearly wrong. Malika asked to stay, repeated the titration twice during the break, and then wrote in her report that the first result was wrong and why, instead of quietly copying the correct value from another group. Ms Saidova said it was the first report in years that began with the word "Incorrect."

What these two moments share, I think, is that Malika is more comfortable with not knowing than most students I have met. She lets a younger student be wrong long enough to learn, and she lets her own result be wrong in writing.

There are things I cannot tell you. I have not seen her in a large lecture or in a setting where she is the only woman in the room, which I understand is common in engineering programmes abroad. She is quiet in large groups, and in her first year of debate club she spoke only twice in a whole term. She has grown more confident since then, but I would not yet call her a natural public speaker, and I do not want to describe her as one.

Malika has also carried responsibilities at home: she looks after two younger sisters after school three days a week. She has never used this as a reason for late work, and I mention it only so that a reader understands why her list of clubs is shorter than some applicants'.

She plans to study engineering. I recommend her because I have watched her teach someone to see a fraction with an apple, and because I believe she will be the student who writes "incorrect" at the top of her own report and then explains why.

Sincerely,
School Counselor
